## Executive Summary

* Copernicus is the EU’s earth observation (EO) programme. Its satellites observe the planet, climate, and environment, and provide free data to the public. The UK, a founding member, left the programme in 2021 after Brexit, before rejoining in 2024\.  

* The government’s new interim evaluation of Copernicus claims that participation has increased the UK’s international influence, secured modest contract returns with signs of growth, and enabled resilient access to highly valued data.  

* Despite this positive spin, Copernicus offers **extremely poor value for money**. Annual costs (around £133 million) far outweigh annual monetised benefits (£63-107 million), with a benefit:cost ratio of 0.47-0.80. While qualitative benefits may exist, they rely heavily on nebulous claims of international influence.  

* Almost all quantified benefits (£56-£100 million) come from improved data access. However, these benefits could well be significantly overstated due to controversial methodological assumptions and sampling bias. Even if the UK left Copernicus, British researchers would still be able to use the open-access data it produces.  

* The small remainder of the benefits (£7 million) comes from the British sector winning delivery contracts. But the value of contracts won is dwarfed 10:1 by the cost of the UK’s contribution, and this gap will realistically never be significantly closed. Copernicus is a very weak industrial support policy.  

* Neither do non-quantitative benefits – primarily ‘influence’ –  justify the membership fee. The UK is cut out of the most important decisions, and there is very little evidence that it is using any informal influence effectively.  

* Other research and development (R\&D) programmes are going through a period of prioritisation as steady government budgets absorb increased cost pressures, and as the government shifts its support towards economic growth. Copernicus should not be exempt from this.  

* The UK will come under significant pressure from the EU to remain a member of Copernicus, as the UK is a significant net contributor to its budgets.  

* **Copernicus does not provide value for money and the UK should seek to withdraw from it**, reallocating the money to one of the litany of R\&D programmes that produce better returns.

## Give me a satellite and £533 million and I shall observe the earth [[TOC: Give me a satellite]]

[Copernicus](https://eu-space.europa.eu/programmes/earth-observation-copernicus-and-eogs) is the European Union’s programme for earth observation. Its grand mission is to look at the earth and its environment “for the benefit of all European citizens”. Sitting inside the EU Space programme, it produces high-quality, free, publicly available earth observation data, which is used by many organisations from climate researchers to urban planners to security services. To do so, it relies on a mix of its own Sentinel satellites, “Contributing Mission” satellites run by other bodies, and a range of ground infrastructure. Copernicus sources its infrastructure from its membership, so companies in member countries get access to potentially lucrative contract competitions. 

When Copernicus was established in 2014, the UK was a founding member. This arrangement lasted until January 2021, when the UK’s transition period ended and it withdrew from the EU. While the UK was outside the programme, British suppliers were cut out of contract competitions, though researchers didn’t lose access to the data Copernicus produces or, thanks to a transitional arrangement, to the additional data benefits membership provides. The UK rejoined Copernicus in January 2024, at the same time as it rejoined the Horizon Europe research collaboration programme. 

The Copernicus membership agreement will cost the UK more than half a billion pounds from 2024-2027, or roughly £133 million annually. This is, clearly, a lot of money. It could buy almost a whole new Francis Crick institute, sovereign compute to the tune of two Isambard-AIs, or more than 100 years of running costs for e-Merlin at Jodrell Bank. If allocated outside of science and tech, it would pay for half a hospital, twenty new schools, or over 800 new prison places. 

So, it matters whether the UK government decides to stay a member of Copernicus under the next funding framework beginning in 2028. The government will make this decision against a complex background. 

* While research and development (R\&D) budgets are increasing modestly over the coming years, the rising costs of research mean that some areas need to be cut back, as particle physicists and the researchers at iconic scientific facilities know [only too well](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0ryevpz48ko). What is the case for Copernicus to be protected from this, especially it is expected to expand over the coming years?   
    
* The government also expects research budgets to bolster anaemic rates of economic growth. Is Copernicus a good way to grow the economy, even though we could access its data without paying for membership?   
    
* Negotiations about Copernicus membership will be intimately connected to negotiations about the UK’s wider relationship with the EU, including our association to the next version of Horizon. Is the UK being part of Copernicus more desirable for the UK or the EU?

To answer these questions, we need to know how our membership of Copernicus is going. Happily, the government has just released an [interim report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluation-of-the-uks-participation-in-the-copernicus-programme) on exactly that.

## The view from altitude

The report’s headline findings all seem rather positive. Although this is only a partial assessment of value for money, there seem to be some fairly substantial benefits. Who wouldn’t want “Increased \[...\] UK influence in EO in Europe and globally”, “modest contract returns, with signs of growth” and “resilient access to highly valued data, including cost savings”? 

On closer inspection, however, the case for the programme rapidly collapses. The headline figures are extremely poor: the annual quantified benefits amount to £63-£107 million, against an annual cost of £133 million. That is a benefit-cost ratio of between 0.47-0.80:1, well below the 1:1 that means you get back as many quantifiable benefits as you spend in cost. This ratio is also far, far lower than the 8:1 figure the government [assesses](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-value-of-public-rd/the-value-of-public-rd) the average public investment in R\&D to yield.[^1] It is hard to see why the government would spend its limited pot of money on a programme that yields such weak returns.

Defenders of Copernicus will say that the quantified benefits of the first two years since rejoining the programme understate the value it will bring in future. There are three main arguments: first, that the data access benefits are real and large; second, that the UK will win more contracts as the Brexit uncertainty wears off; and third, that quantified benefit-cost ratios are only part of the story, and that the non-quantified benefits are so important that they justify staying a member. Unfortunately, none of these arguments hold up.  Let us examine why, looking at each in turn.

## Back down to earth

### Data access

Of the quantified benefits Copernicus has produced for the UK (£63-107 million), almost all (£56-100 million) come from data access. This is mysterious. The open-access data the programme produces – images of the earth, and so on – are available to researchers basically anywhere, whether their country is a member or not. The open access, then, doesn’t count as a benefit of membership. That comes from somewhere else: being part of Copernicus reduces some practical barriers UK researchers might face in accessing data. Member nations avoid usage restrictions, including download quotas, delays, and archive access limits. They also gain access to independent data assurance and public sector access to the full suite of data, including some “restricted” data that is not available under open access. Lastly, member nations can discuss what data is collected to try to meet their country’s needs. The evaluation estimates that these additional benefits – not access, but the full and streamlined nature of access – are collectively worth up to £100 million. How did they arrive at this figure?

First, evaluators asked a small number of institutions how much they would be willing to pay to avoid losing certain features of the data Copernicus provides, such as spatial resolution or bandwidth caps. A small, self-selecting sample of those institutions actually answered. The evaluators then extrapolated their answers across all institutions that use Copernicus data. Their analysis implies that researchers would pay £110 million to avoid the loss of all access to Copernicus data, £100 million to avoid loss of high spatial resolution, £56 million to avoid bandwidth caps, and so on. The evaluators then assumed that the UK *would* lose the additional benefits if it left Copernicus, so estimated the value of the data benefits of the UK’s membership at £56-100 million. This calculation is fully in accordance with the Green Book process the government prefers. It is also fatally flawed, for two reasons: 

1. **Many researchers will overstate how much they would pay to avoid downgrades.** They know that a programme that gives them free data is being evaluated, and they have every incentive to defend it. Even the evaluators admit that “It is possible that respondents could overstate their hypothetical willingness to avoid a loss of Copernicus data.” Moreover, the few organisations who chose to answer the survey might be expected to place a higher value on Copernicus than those who ignored the survey, so generalising from their preferences is too generous to the programme. Simply put, the benefits figures are probably overestimates.

2. **Moreover, we can’t be certain that leaving Copernicus would limit our access in all the ways this analysis assumes.** The report notes that when the UK last left the programme, “*many users did not notice data constraints and were even unaware that the UK had withdrawn*”. To be fair, they were protected by a temporary UK-EU agreement, which likely wouldn’t be repeated if the UK left once and for all. But it’s unduly pessimistic to assume that the UK would lose many of the elements that respondents cared about most. Some losses, like lower bandwidth caps, would happen automatically in the absence of a bespoke agreement; but some, like narrower geographical coverage, are merely possible future scenarios that could emerge should the UK not have a seat at the table. The report itself concedes that “defining a suitable counterfactual is challenging”. Critically, the losses that are guaranteed to happen are primarily those researchers cared least about. For example, the UK would certainly lose access to restricted data if it left Copernicus, but this would have a very limited impact: only 11% of respondents reported knowingly accessing restricted Copernicus data at all.

There is no doubt that the UK’s earth observation industry benefits from having full and streamlined access to Copernicus. But there is good reason to think that these figures overestimate that benefit. The benefits estimate rests on what a few dozen respondents claim they would hypothetically pay from budgets they don’t have to avoid scenarios that leaving Copernicus may not even cause. Even with this overestimate, the costs of Copernicus still far outweigh the benefits. 

### Contracts

As far as benefits go, contracts are a sideshow. The evaluation finds that the net economic benefit to the UK in terms of gross value added is just £7 million annually. This comes from UK organisations winning £26.1 million worth of contracts across two years, a figure that pales in comparison to the roughly £250 million the UK government has sent to the EU. A plurality of the contracts won have gone to public bodies: the Met Office has won 12 of the UK’s 61 contracts, worth £6.6 million, and RAL Space, the UK’s national lab for space, has won three more. If Copernicus is supposed to be an industrial support measure, it is an extremely bad one: **for every £10 the government has to support the sector, it is effectively giving them £1 in a paperwork-heavy, public-sector-biased way, while ritually immolating the other £9.** Even if, as there is some evidence to suggest, £1 of an EU contract is so prestigious it is worth more than £1 of UK cash, it is not so prestigious that it is worth more than £10 of UK cash.

There are two principal responses to this. Neither is adequate. Contracts will always remain a sideshow. 

First, perhaps things look particularly bad because we joined in the middle of the programme, when many of the contracts have been allocated. While this might be true up to a point, the UK has never got back anything close to today’s fee. In fact, the UK’s performance has now recovered to a level  comparable to its pre-Brexit performance, so it would be unwise to bet on a much better outcome.

Second, perhaps the UK’s share of contracts will increase over time. After all, we have already seen almost 30% growth from 2024 to 2025, and the UK now has the benefit of post-Brexit stability. It is indeed possible, even likely, that there will be a moderate increase in the UK’s contract returns. However, even on the impossibly optimistic assumptions that the cost of the programme never increases and that the UK’s share of contracts continues to grow explosively, **it would still take more than a decade to reach the point where the UK’s cumulative contract returns match its cumulative contributions**. By that point, we would need to be winning contracts worth three-quarters of Copernicus’ entire budget. In other words, contracts will never justify our Copernicus membership.

### Influence

The quantitative case for Copernicus membership clearly does not stack up. Once we remove the highly uncertain benefits resulting from data access, **we are left with a truly abysmal benefit cost ratio of around 0.1:1.** 

<Figure src="/assets/images/writing/the-earth-observation-black-hole/03.png" caption="Source: UKSA/DSIT, Copernicus Evaluation Programme Interim Report (Aug 2026). Government estimate: £56–100m data-use benefit (willingness-to-pay survey) plus £7m/yr contract GVA. Sceptical estimate: contract GVA (£7m) + avoided storage and reprocessing (£2.5–4m) + allowance for restricted services and quota/latency effects."/>

Not all that matters can be measured, of course, and benefit-cost ratios are not everything. It would be a mistake to require all scientific programmes to break even, and state intervention is often most justified in cases where the monetary benefits are of secondary importance. But in this case, the financial situation is truly dire, and the programme focuses on data production rather than innovation itself, so the non-quantified benefits would need to be very large to justify our membership fee. Unfortunately, they are not. 

The report trumpets international influence as the key qualitative benefit. It states that “participation today is a long-term decision which enables the UK to have a voice in shaping one of the world’s most advanced EO systems. It ensures that data of UK interest is captured, \[...\] and that UK industry can play a role in developing these leading capabilities.” Decisions about data capture and the direction of the programme are certainly important, as is the opportunity to keep the programme’s outputs open access. But the UK lacks formal voting rights, there are “limited concrete examples” of the UK shaping programme decisions, and “there is a perception within the UK EO community that objectives driving UK participation in Copernicus are not clear”. This is hardly encouraging. Moreover, “Views on the extent to which the UK currently benefits from access and influence differ”, with European stakeholders much happier than UK stakeholders. Well might they be; UK taxpayers are paying hundreds of millions of pounds to European companies to produce data that the UK could use for free.

## Continental drift

That brings us to the rub. Copernicus is a pawn in a game of chess the UK is playing with the EU. It is an afterthought: even DSIT’s (now BIST’s) R\&D [budgets](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dsit-research-and-development-plans-to-2029-to-2030/dsit-research-and-development-rd-plans-to-20292030) wrap it in with Horizon in a line called “UK contribution to EU programmes”, since it is less than one-tenth the size of its brother. Meanwhile, the Burnham government seems just as keen as its predecessor on closer ties with the EU, and the Europeans know this. If the UK wants to have a closer trading relationship, closer working on migration or energy or Ukraine, the EU can make this conditional, implicitly or explicitly, on our continued support for Copernicus, just as they did with [Erasmus](https://www.ersatzben.com/p/erasmus-and-the-governments-opportunity). Escape is possible – despite European wishes, the UK is no longer part of Euratom, the nuclear cooperation equivalent – but it is difficult when the UK is simultaneously negotiating its future relationship with the EU, including on Horizon.

If Copernicus membership is primarily a sop to the EU, a tribute paid for access to Horizon and closer political ties, **the government needs to recognise that and negotiate accordingly**, while internally recognising Copernicus as a liability. This means starting from a negotiating position of withdrawing from Copernicus. If a concession is necessary, it means pushing strongly for a Horizon-style clawback mechanism if the value UK suppliers win in contracts falls below an agreed floor. Without this, the UK will continue to pay for other countries' industrial success while  struggling to do the same domestically.

## Ground truth

The authors of the evaluation report do not hide their preferred outcome. They worry about the increasing cost of the programme as it undergoes a planned expansion only because it could pose “a risk to future UK participation.” On the contrary: given the value for money Copernicus offers, future UK participation is itself a risk to be avoided, and cost increases are to be welcomed if they save us from that. 

Even though the write-up has been framed as positively as possible – and more positively than is credible – the underlying evaluation of Copernicus shows unequivocally that the case for membership is a very weak one indeed, even relying on optimistic assumptions about the benefits that participation brings. The government must take that position seriously and negotiate with the EU accordingly. In straightened fiscal circumstances, the government could spend £500 million so much better than this.

[^1]:  This figure includes spillovers, so the comparison is not apples to apples. But industrial R\&D and tech procurement contracts might have fewer spillovers than early-mid stage R\&D, as the ideas are more easily excludable, so it is unlikely the gap will be made up in Copernicus’ favour.
